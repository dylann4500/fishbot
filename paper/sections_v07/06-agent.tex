\section{FishBot v0.7}
\label{sec:agent}

\fishseven{} is a configuration of the \vsix{} policy family rather than a new architecture. That
sentence was written into the evaluation protocol before any holdout result was known, and the
evaluation did not disturb it. This section describes the agent conceptually. The literal
configuration string, its option map, the parameter vector layout and the freeze artifact's hashes
are in Appendix~\ref{app:parameters}.

\subsection{Inference and the ask and declaration policy}
\label{sec:agent-policy}

Inference is unchanged from \vsix{} and is the object of \S\ref{sec:inference}: the exact
observer-conditioned deal posterior, with the deployed path applying a fitted policy prior over it.

The acting policy is a fitted linear score over features of the belief and the public state. An ask
decision scores each legal (target, card) pair and takes the maximum; a declaration decision consults
the named-allocation probability the inference object supplies and commits when a value rule judges
the claim worth making. \fishseven{} inherits the \vsix{} parameter vector unchanged, and
\textbf{no fit of any kind was applied to it during this cycle}. The vector was found by \vsix{}'s
repaired optimiser under a minimax-regret objective over a four-member opponent panel. Of the
\vsevenCoordinates{} coordinates in the extended vector, a number are known to be inert, and
Appendix~\ref{app:parameters} lists which and why.

\subsection{Tie-breaking that preserves common knowledge}
\label{sec:agent-tiebreak}

At \vsevenTieContested\% of contested ask decisions two or more candidates score bit-for-bit
identically (\S\ref{sec:background-vsix}), and in \vsix{} the winner was whichever the enumerator
emitted first, under an unstable sort. That is a correctness problem rather than a strength problem:
the choice is arbitrary, but it is also unpredictable, and \S\ref{sec:game-why} explains why
unpredictability of the wrong kind is expensive here. Three identical deterministic teammates
coordinate for free only if each can compute what the others will do; a tie broken by an
implementation detail is not something a teammate can reconstruct.

\fishseven{} replaces enumeration order with a hash of the public event stream
(\texttt{rtie}). Every teammate observes that stream, so every teammate can compute the same
tie-break, and no seat's private hand influences it. The mechanism is a coordination repair that
happens to be free, and \S\ref{sec:development-tie} reports what happened when a later experiment
tried to do better than randomising within the tied set.

\subsection{Half-suit contestation and information denial}
\label{sec:agent-contest}

The one component that carries measurable strength is a single coordinate weighting how willing the
agent is to contest a half-suit in which an opponent has demonstrated certainty
(\texttt{r12}, internally \texttt{oppCertDonate}). Raising it makes the agent less willing to ask into
positions where the ask would confirm information the opponents can use, and more willing to
contest half-suits whose ownership is still genuinely open.

Two facts about its provenance matter for how the results in \S\ref{sec:results-attribution} should
be read. It was found by an \emph{unfitted} coordinate sweep during the adversary-generation phase,
not by architecture work: a single hand-set value, with no fitting, measured at
\vsevenOneSwitchArm{}~pp against the deployed policy over \vsevenPhaseTwoCell{} games. And its edge
against the more expensive frontier configuration \fmid{} is \vsevenOneSwitchArmVsFmid{}~pp, which is
negative, so it is a strength gain with a worst case rather than an exploit
(\S\ref{sec:development-adversaries}).

\subsection{Removing the urgency escalation}
\label{sec:agent-urgency}

\vsix{} inherited a declaration-urgency escalation keyed to an event counter. Past a threshold of 220
public events the declaration rule lowers its team-ownership floor and begins cashing half-suits at
close to a coin flip, which costs \vsevenCliff{}~pp to a policy held in that regime. No adversary
reaches it, since the longest game any adversarial search produced against an unmodified opponent is
\vsevenCliffIncumbentTail{} events, but the machinery is defective in ordinary play as well:
\S\ref{sec:development-alloc} reports that removing it eliminates \vsevenLoneUrgencyShare\% of the
entire declaration allocation-error class. \fishseven{} disables it
(\texttt{pool}, \texttt{oppfloor}, \texttt{force}, \texttt{askfloor}).

The escalation was, however, also the policy's termination guarantee, so removing it creates an
obligation.

\subsection{A stall detector as the replacement termination guarantee}
\label{sec:agent-stall}

\fishseven{} escalates after twelve consecutive public events in which this seat's hard-certificate
hash has not changed (\texttt{stall}). The quantity being watched is the seat's own deduction state,
so the rule fires exactly when the game has stopped making progress from that seat's point of view,
which is the condition the event counter was a proxy for.

The threshold was set from a measurement rather than by taste. Over \vsevenStallMirrorGames{} mirror
games, \vsix{} never produces a no-progress run longer than \vsevenStallIncumbentRun{}, while a
deliberately deadlocked configuration, the negative control of \S\ref{sec:evaluation-gate}, produces
\vsevenStallFrozenRun{}. Any threshold between 12 and 60 is therefore simultaneously unreachable in
ordinary play and immediate in a deadlock. Armed, the rule's mirror transcript digest is byte
identical to the same configuration without it: its cost in ordinary play is not small but identically
zero, because it never runs. \S\ref{sec:results-attribution} confirms that on holdout material.

\subsection{Guarded determinized test-time search}
\label{sec:agent-search}

\fishseven{} enables the search that \vsix{} built, measured and then deployed switched off
(\texttt{s1}, with \texttt{det}, \texttt{cand}, \texttt{kappa}, \texttt{rbelief}, \texttt{depth},
\texttt{maxq}). At a searched decision the agent samples twelve full deals from its own posterior and
plays each forward with all six continuation players reconstructed at their own information sets,
rather than made clairvoyant.

Two design choices are what make it usable. First, the deviation rule is a \emph{paired
lower-confidence bound}: the search departs from the blueprint's choice only when the sampled
advantage of an alternative clears a shrinkage threshold, which is what converts the
\vsixCurseGap{}~pp optimiser's curse of \S\ref{sec:background-vsix} into a usable mechanism. Second,
the search is endgame-truncated, running only when at most a set number of cards remain unresolved,
which is what keeps its cost at a small multiple of the blueprint rather than the orders of magnitude
the unrestricted configuration costs (\S\ref{sec:results-cost}).

\subsection{Coordination assumptions, and one defect the evaluation runs under}
\label{sec:agent-coordination}

\fishseven{} deploys as three identical agents with no shared secret and no communication beyond
public actions. Its coordination is therefore common knowledge by the argument of
\S\ref{sec:game-why}, provided every component is deterministic given public state and own hand,
which the tie-break repair of \S\ref{sec:agent-tiebreak} is what secures.

One engine defect is present in every number in this paper and is stated rather than buried. Agents
are constructed once per worker thread and reused across the deals that thread is given, and some
per-agent state survives the inter-deal reset, reachable only under the search. The carrier was
identified as the rollout engine, which the reset never touches, but the specific field was not.
It was deliberately not repaired, because the one-line fix changes the play of every searching
configuration by roughly one deal in \vsevenSsixIncidence{}, and applying it after the configuration
was frozen would defeat the purpose of freezing. \S\ref{sec:results-controls} measures its magnitude
on holdout material.
